I do not know how intelligent AI will become. Looking at the changes already happening, though, model performance seems unlikely to be the only thing that changes.

Today, using AI usually means opening a chat and typing a prompt. In ten years, will we still explain our circumstances every time, find the right apps, and operate them one by one? I suspect we will not. As AI moves closer to people and learns more about them, our relationship with devices may change. That connects models to interfaces, devices, energy, and the people using them.

## AI grows larger, but also moves closer to us

Recent AI progress has relied on larger models and more computation. Large LLMs will probably remain important for complex reasoning and processing substantial information. But everyday tasks do not always need a huge model. Finding something in personal files, understanding the current task, operating an app, or lightly interpreting the surroundings may often be possible with a smaller model.

On a phone or computer, response time, energy consumption, memory, and privacy matter alongside raw ability. Making SLMs smarter while keeping them small is an important direction in its own right.

Apple Intelligence offers a useful example along that path: on-device models combined with Private Cloud Compute when more processing is required. Different work goes to different models and places.

This might become more common. A small local AI handles ordinary tasks and passes difficult ones to a cloud LLM. The user experiences one assistant, even though several models are involved. Beyond speed and efficiency, keeping an AI on the device also makes personal context easier to handle.

## Explaining ourselves less often

Even using ChatGPT now, I find it tedious to explain past conversations and my current circumstances again. Simply remembering that I prefer a certain explanation or have already thought through part of a problem changes the experience considerably.

Today, people still enter much of that context themselves. Personal AI becoming part of everyday life could change this. With permission, it could draw on calendars, email, files, previous conversations, location, the current screen, and information from wearables.

Ask what to do before tomorrow’s test, and an assistant that knows the date, your recent study progress, and the questions you keep getting wrong can answer for your situation rather than offer generic study advice.

AI’s ability would then depend not only on how much it knows about the world, but also on how well it understands this person right now. It might maintain a model of someone’s knowledge, plans, preferences, actions, and physical condition. I do not mean copying an entire human, but something closer to a digital twin containing the state needed to support them.

What interests me most is what follows. With enough context, AI can change more than its answers. It can change the screen itself.

## Context can change the interface

Our devices assume we will find the functions we need: a calendar for plans, a map for places, a transit app for trains. We work out the goal and choose the tools.

If AI understands the situation, it need not always show the same interface. Before leaving home, it could combine the next appointment, travel time, weather, and things to take. While studying, it could prioritize the current material, remaining time, recent mistakes, and progress.

Instead of adapting ourselves to a fixed interface, the interface adapts to our circumstances. Such adaptive UI could also soften the boundaries between apps. The AI could use different services in the background and assemble the information needed at that moment.

That does not mean removing UI. As AI makes more judgments, people need to understand what it thinks it knows, why it is showing something, and what it is about to do.

If it proposes moving an appointment by 30 minutes, it helps to see its reasons: perhaps short sleep and enough room before the next appointment. People also need to control what the AI can do before it acts.

UI would serve not only to operate devices, but also to inspect AI’s understanding, judgments, and authority. To me, that is the most important part of this change.

## Beyond one screen, beyond one device

Once AI can decide what to show, it may also choose where to show it. Walking down a street does not require a large screen. A single arrow in glasses might be enough for directions. A watch might suit a quick schedule check, and a computer would still suit complex work.

The next step after the smartphone may therefore be more than one device replacing it. Watches, earbuds, glasses, phones, and computers could connect to one personal AI. It would use context to decide which device should show which information, and how much.

The next generation might be several devices distributed around one person. AI, UI, and hardware would become parts of the same discussion.

## What powers that future?

The closer AI stays to us, the harder it becomes to ignore energy. Large cloud models and local SLMs both need power. Small watches and glasses cannot simply carry ever larger batteries to run sensors and AI all day.

Smaller models and efficient chips become important. Doing the same work with less energy should count as part of performance.

At a talk I attended, PlayStation creator Ken Kutaragi mentioned how interesting it would be if something like proteins could become a power source. That stayed with me.

This does not mean phones will soon run on protein. But research already explores harvesting small amounts of energy from biological materials, bodily movement, and the environment. If sensors and SLMs become much more efficient, sources too weak today might become useful.

Thinking about AI soon leads into chips, sensors, devices, and energy. These subjects are difficult to separate.

## What happens to people?

These changes could bring convenience and substantial differences in access. Some people might have an assistant that understands their knowledge and plans, surfaces information early, and operates services. Others might still search and open apps one at a time.

We already see differences in how AI users gather and organize information. Personal AI might widen that gap. Does this mean people without technical knowledge will be unable to keep up?

I do not think it is that simple. Mature technology can become easier for people who know less about it. Using a phone does not require knowledge of CPUs or communication standards. Well-designed personal AI might remove the need to learn dozens of apps. People could state their goal and let the AI connect them to the necessary functions.

Yet AI also holds personal information and makes some judgments for us. Ease of use must coexist with understanding what it knows and how much authority it has. Reconciling those may be harder than improving the model itself.

## Talking about the future

LLMs could grow while SLMs become smaller and closer to people. Context makes personal AI and digital-twin-like systems more plausible. That context changes interfaces, which can distribute information across devices. Keeping everything running brings us back to efficient chips and new ways of obtaining energy.

Together, these could produce a very different digital environment.

My main question is more than how intelligent it becomes. How much do we allow AI to know about us? How well can we understand its decisions? Will technical experts receive most of the benefit, or can it become something anyone can use without thinking about the machinery?

What I want to keep exploring is how that development changes the relationship between people and technology.
