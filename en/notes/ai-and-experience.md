The main film begins with Sam confronting the feeling that AI is overtaking filmmaking skills he spent years developing. During a real journey through Mauritania, he introduces an AI-generated fictional robot called Kevin and asks what it means to keep creating.

These two films made me wonder whether the value we seek in human-made work changes as more things can be generated.

By non-generated content, I do not mean work that uses no AI. Both films use it. My question is whether the work is grounded in someone’s actual experience. Perhaps the meaning of non-generated will gradually shift in that direction.

Sam also discusses surveillance, job losses, training data used without consent, and environmental impact. Those matter, but this essay focuses on one question: the value of expression grounded in experience.

## Intention is the first distinction

“AI slop” refers to low-quality generated content. But is the discomfort behind that term only about visual mistakes and unnatural writing?

Even if those problems disappear, I think something can still feel lacking when we cannot see why it was made. It may look impressive without revealing who thought about it or why they wanted to communicate it.

In the making-of film, Sam describes receiving an obviously AI-written email that felt impersonal. Polished writing and feeling that someone has engaged with you are different things. Along with the words, we receive the sense that someone took time to think and choose them for us.

The same may apply to creative work. Using AI can involve intention, choosing an expression, discovering something in an unexpected output, and reconsidering an idea. Intention and discovery are not exclusive to non-generated work. Conversely, something made without AI can feel empty if it exists only for views or superficially imitates another work. The absence of AI does not guarantee honesty or originality.

So the first distinction is whether there is intention, rather than whether something was generated.

But expression grounded in experience has another dimension that intention alone cannot supply.

I do not think intention is sufficient by itself.

Relying on tools is nothing new. Sam describes seeing this change throughout his life. In 2011, a slow-motion camera cost $100,000, so he used a $300 plugin. In 2020, virtual instruments let him reproduce orchestral sounds at a keyboard without playing those instruments. In 2022, Magic Mask removed tedious manual masking. In 2026, Higgsfield enables VFX he could not have made alone.

Cheaper equipment and lower technical barriers let more people create. As he says, that trend is not unique to AI.

The question comes when the experience itself becomes optional. In the making-of film, Sam imagines generating an entire travel film without going anywhere: no adventure, no actual memories, everything finished at a computer. He says that is not what he wants. Experiencing things and recording them with a camera is meaningful to him.

A tool replacing part of an experience and a tool allowing us to omit the whole experience belong to a continuous change, but cross different thresholds. The latter removes the need to place our body and time somewhere in the world.

Obtaining the image you wanted and spending the time needed to capture it are not the same experience. Heat tires you. A late train makes you wait. People you meet have their own circumstances and opinions. Weather, places, and people do not reorganize themselves around the film you want. An uncontrollable reality changes your thoughts and actions.

Unexpected AI outputs can also change an initial plan, but they arise inside a creative environment you have set up. Traveling brings you into contact with reality outside it. You might set out to film a landscape and become interested in someone who lives there. Plans failing might draw your attention to something you did not intend to notice. Rather than merely fit reality to your theme, you must rethink your questions in response to reality.

That is the part intention alone cannot replace. Generating a journey does not make you a participant in those events. Being exposed to an uncontrollable world, and being changed by it, is another path.

The value of experience therefore lies on a second axis beyond intention. This is particularly important in travel and documentary work, where “this actually happened” is part of the value itself.

## Proof of work: clues and limits

In the making-of film, Sam suggests non-generated work may gain value, especially when the process is documented. He calls this “proof of work.”

I understand that as a way for viewers to receive the creator’s experience as part of the work, rather than as a measure of how much they suffered.

The relationship between the two films shows this in practice. Sam and Daniel calculate afterward how much of the finished film was actually AI-generated. Their figure is around 15% of all pixels. Because its appearance might suggest a larger generated share, they disclose the breakdown. Showing the process can mean accumulating these ordinary, specific disclosures.

The main film confronts changes to Sam’s skills and position. The making-of shows the travel, filming, and experimentation with AI required to turn that question into a work. Knowing the process changes how we see the film. The desert becomes an actual place where Sam thought about change, rather than only a backdrop.

But another problem remains. If a finished film can be generated, so can apparent behind-the-scenes footage, an account of an experience, or even a figure such as 15%. Once proof of work itself can be generated, a single record becomes difficult to rely on.

Viewers must then look for consistency across clues. Does the account agree with other footage? How clearly are live-action and generated parts distinguished? Has the creator consistently been honest about that distinction in earlier work?

Proof of work gains meaning through a history of disclosure, rather than a single piece of evidence.

This also creates an obligation for creators. If experience is part of the value they claim, they take responsibility for communicating it honestly. Extending the work to include its making also extends what is evaluated and what can be lost if trust is broken. Proof of work is both evidence of an experience and a promise to keep disclosing to the same standard.

## Experience does not replace the finished work

These films use AI, so they are not non-generated in a simple sense. Still, using AI does not erase the value of traveling and filming in the real world.

What remains valuable in human-made work seems grounded in both intentional choices and, in certain genres, committing one’s body and time to an uncontrollable reality. Without the former, generated and non-generated work alike can feel empty. Without the latter, the premise of travel and documentary work is unsettled even if intention is present.

Previously, experience was necessary to make the work. Perhaps we will increasingly watch someone’s work to encounter what they experienced. Generating a similar image does not replace the experience of the person who went to capture it.

Rather than compete with generated work on polish alone, work grounded in experience can show whose reality we are encountering, with traces of both intention and experience visible.
