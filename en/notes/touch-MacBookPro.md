On October 28, 2026, Apple announced MacBook Pro with PROLED XDR Display. There are many changes, including tandem OLED and Dynamic Island. The one I want to focus on is direct touch interaction with the display, a first for Mac.

![MacBook Pro with PROLED XDR Display](../images/notes/touch-MacBookPro-3.jpg)
*MacBook Pro with PROLED XDR Display*

Apple has long resisted touchscreens on Mac. Following this announcement, I have seen criticism that the company is contradicting Steve Jobs’s earlier comments.

I was against touch on MacBook too. I thought it would blur the distinction between MacBook and iPad. But this announcement changed my mind. MacBook’s main way of working stays familiar, and touch joins it as an additional interaction. I would welcome that.

I also have doubts about criticism based only on Apple’s past statements. An iPad with Magic Keyboard has an upright display and still supports touch. Why should that be a problem specifically on MacBook?

## Reaching up for long periods is uncomfortable

At the Back to the Mac event on October 20, 2010, Steve Jobs explained Apple’s objection to touchscreen notebooks. The company had tried them, but continually reaching toward a nearly vertical display tired the arm. The conclusion was that notebook displays were not suited to touch.

I still understand that point. Reaching toward the screen every time you edit text or manage files would be tiring. MacBook has an excellent trackpad that handles many tasks without taking your hands far from the keyboard. For sustained work, it is a sensible choice.

But supporting touch does not require doing everything by touching the screen. Use the trackpad normally, and reach out when direct interaction is useful. Arm fatigue remains an issue, but it does not remove every reason to add touch.

## What about iPad and Magic Keyboard?

Magic Keyboard for iPad Pro, introduced in 2020, lets the iPad stand in a notebook-like position and adds a keyboard and trackpad.

![iPad with Magic Keyboard](../images/notes/touch-MacBookPro-2.jpg)
*iPad with Magic Keyboard*

That looks quite similar to a MacBook. Touching its screen also means taking a hand off the keyboard and extending an arm. If touching a nearly vertical screen is tiring, the same problem should apply here.

The original designs differ, of course. iPadOS is built around touch, with buttons and gestures sized for fingers. macOS developed around a mouse and trackpad. The iPad can also be detached and held, so the conditions are not identical.

Even so, the act of reaching toward an upright screen is similar. When Apple added a trackpad to iPad, it kept touch. Use the trackpad for long sessions or precise work and touch when direct interaction is preferable. Adding Magic Keyboard did not make touch unnecessary.

An upright display and direct touch already coexist on iPad. Why should MacBook not offer a similar choice?

## How does the MacBook experience change?

The question is macOS’s interface. Small buttons and menu items designed for a pointer can be difficult to use with fingers.

The new MacBook Pro addresses that. When you touch the screen, menu spacing increases and window controls grow. The usual Mac interface remains available, while touch interaction brings controls better suited to fingers. Existing apps can treat touch as a click, and developers have APIs for touch-specific interactions.

That is interesting in light of my earlier concern about the distinction from iPad.

If macOS became primarily a touch interface, the distinction might become unclear. Here, though, the traditional Mac workflow remains, and touch supplements it when needed. That is the part I appreciate.

MacBook already has a Multi-Touch trackpad for scrolling, pinching, and other gestures. Even with those abilities, directly touching the thing you want to manipulate offers a different kind of ease.

App development is one example. Developers previously reproduced iPhone and iPad touch interactions with a mouse or trackpad in the Mac simulator. Apple now demonstrates testing those interactions by touching the MacBook Pro display directly.

The screen size and the way you hold the device differ from an actual iPhone or iPad. Still, checking interactions with a finger during development could bring testing closer to real use. Having touch available alongside the mouse and trackpad seems useful.

There are also moments when using an iPhone or iPad every day makes you instinctively reach for the MacBook screen. Making that screen respond is a simple change that could improve usability.

For long writing sessions and precise positioning, keep using the trackpad and keyboard. When you want to touch something directly, use touch. Being able to choose according to the task is what I find appealing.

![App development on MacBook Pro with PROLED XDR Display](../images/notes/touch-MacBookPro-4.jpg)

## A MacBook that responds when you want to touch it

Apple has changed a long-standing position, and the arm fatigue Jobs described has not disappeared. But discomfort during extended touch use and the value of including a touchscreen are separate questions.

Magic Keyboard added a trackpad to a product centered on touch. This MacBook Pro adds touch to a product centered on a trackpad. Their available interactions have become more similar, while their primary experiences remain distinct.

Apple also continues to sell a non-touch MacBook Pro with Liquid Retina XDR display alongside the touch-capable PROLED XDR model. People who want touch can choose it; people satisfied with the existing experience can choose the other model. The choice exists across both interaction methods and the product range.

I used to oppose touch on MacBook because I feared the roles of iPad and MacBook would become unclear. Seeing touch added without replacing the MacBook workflow has made me more positive.

The observation that reaching up for Multi-Touch is uncomfortable does not automatically lead to the conclusion that MacBook has no use for touch.

A MacBook without a touchscreen has rarely caused me serious trouble. Even so, there are moments when I want to touch the display directly. This addition responds to that desire. What I once considered unnecessary now seems worth welcoming.
